# [[Projects/Languages/Slovura/Slovura Lessons/Slovura 04 - Compound Words\|Slovura 04 - Compound Words]]

[[Projects/Languages/Slovura/Slovura\|Slovura]] has a small set of basic [[Projects/Languages/Slovura/Slovura Lessons/Slovura 01 - Slovura Roots\|roots]], but those roots can be combined to express much more specific concepts.

Unlike a normal phrase, a **compound word is written as a single word**. Its roots form an internal structure following the same basic idea as a sentence:

> [!note] Compound structure
> **Head + Predicate**

A compound can therefore be thought of as a **miniature sentence compressed into one word**.

- **The First Syllable (The Head):** This is the "subject" of the word. It answers: _What is this thing fundamentally?_
- **The Remainder (The Predicate):** The second syllable after the first forms the main part of the predicate. Every following syllable modifies the second syllable.

## Head

The **first root** is the head of the compound. It tells you what kind of thing the entire word fundamentally refers to.

If the first root means _person_, the whole compound describes some kind of person. If it means _colour_, the whole compound describes some kind of colour.

## Predicate

Everything after the head describes it.

In a compound with three or more roots, the second root forms the head of the predicate and later roots describe that root.

The structure is therefore not simply:

> A + B + C + D

but:

> **A + ((B + C) + D)**

This grouping is important because it tells you which concept modifies which.

In practice, compounds of four or more roots are uncommon.

## Example: `kaklama`

> [!example] [[Projects/Languages/Slovura/Slovura Words/kaklama\|kaklama]]
> **[[Projects/Languages/Slovura/Slovura Roots/ka\|ka]] + ([[Projects/Languages/Slovura/Slovura Roots/kla\|kla]] + [[Projects/Languages/Slovura/Slovura Roots/ma\|ma]])**
>
> person + (head/control + land)
>
> a person associated with controlling or leading a land
>
> **meaning: ruler / head of state**

The first root tells us that `kaklama` is fundamentally a **person**. The remaining roots describe what kind of person.

## Example: `kapusta`

> [!example] [[Projects/Languages/Slovura/Slovura Words/kapusta\|kapusta]]
> **[[Projects/Languages/Slovura/Slovura Roots/ka\|ka]] + ([[Projects/Languages/Slovura/Slovura Roots/pu\|pu]] + [[Projects/Languages/Slovura/Slovura Roots/sta\|sta]])**
>
> person + (mouth + big)
>
> a person with a big mouth
> 
> **meaning: overconfident or indiscreet person**

Again, `ka` establishes that the compound describes a person, while `pusta` describes that person.

## Compounds Are Descriptions

A compound does not have to be an established dictionary word before a speaker can use it.

By joining roots, the speaker asks the listener to understand them together as **one concept**. The listener can interpret a new compound from its component roots and context.

This means compound words do not form a second vocabulary that must all be memorized. Their internal structure remains meaningful.

## Root Boundaries

Because Slovura roots are monosyllabic and end in vowels, they can be concatenated directly without inserting extra sounds.

For example:

> `ka` + `kla` + `ma` → **kaklama**

The roots remain the same when they become part of a compound.

## Compound Words vs. Root Words

Only the basic roots need to be learned as fundamental vocabulary. Compound words are built from those roots.

Lists of established or useful Slovura words should therefore be treated as examples and convenient vocabulary, not as an entirely separate set of basic roots that every speaker must memorize.

## Remember

> [!summary]
> - Several roots can be joined into one Slovura word.
> - Compounds follow **Head + Predicate** structure.
> - The first root identifies the fundamental concept.
> - The remaining roots describe that concept.
> - Longer compounds are internally grouped rather than being a flat list of roots.
> - New compounds can be understood from their roots and context.
